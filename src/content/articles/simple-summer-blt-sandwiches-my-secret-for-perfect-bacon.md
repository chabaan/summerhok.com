<p>The first bite of a perfect BLT is a study in contrasts: cool, crisp lettuce against warm, salty bacon; the creamy tang of mayonnaise meeting the sweet-acid burst of a ripe tomato. It's a sandwich so simple that its success rests entirely on technique. And the most common mistake? Soggy bread. Let me walk you through the method I use every summer — one that guarantees a BLT that actually holds together.</p>
<figure><img src="https://cdn.midjourney.com/90d0882a-3fa3-4ebc-b60c-a55e39cb5249/0_2.png" alt="Crispy oven-baked bacon on a wire rack over a foil-lined baking sheet, just out of the oven" /></figure>


<h2>The Bacon Method: Oven, Not Stovetop</h2>
<p>Frying bacon in a skillet works, but it's uneven. The strips in the center cook faster, you're left with a greasy stovetop, and the bacon often curls into a cupped shape that doesn't lay flat on your sandwich. Oven-baking solves all of that.</p>
<p>Preheat your oven to 400°F (200°C). Line a rimmed baking sheet with aluminum foil — this makes cleanup trivial. If you have a wire rack that fits inside the sheet, use it. The rack lifts the bacon so hot air circulates underneath, giving you flat, evenly cooked strips. No rack? No problem. Lay the bacon directly on the foil, but flip each strip halfway through.</p>
<p>Here's the timing I rely on: for thick-cut bacon, 14 minutes yields a chewy-crisp edge with a slightly tender center. At 16 minutes, the fat renders fully and the meat tightens. At 18 minutes, you get a shatter-crisp strip that breaks cleanly when bent. I pull mine at 16 minutes — the sweet spot. Transfer the bacon to a paper-towel-lined plate immediately. The residual heat finishes the job.</p>
<p><strong>Common mistake to avoid:</strong> Don't crowd the pan. Overlapping strips steam rather than crisp. Give each slice its own space.</p>

<h2>The Tomato: Patience Pays</h2>
<p>A BLT lives or dies on the tomato. Out of season, it's a sad thing. In summer, when tomatoes are heavy and fragrant, it's the star. Choose a beefsteak or heirloom variety that's deeply red and gives slightly at the stem end.</p>
<p>Slice off the top and bottom — just a thin disc — then cut the rest into even slices about 1/3-inch thick. Lay these slices on a paper towel for exactly 2 minutes. This step is non-negotiable. That brief rest draws out surface moisture that would otherwise soak into your bread within seconds of assembly. After 2 minutes, season the slices with a tiny pinch of flaky sea salt. Salt draws out even more moisture, but more importantly, it makes the tomato taste like the best version of itself.</p>

<h2>The Lettuce: Dry Is the Goal</h2>
<p>Wash your lettuce leaves and spin them dry in a salad spinner, then lay them on a clean kitchen towel and pat the tops dry. Any water clinging to the leaves will mix with the mayonnaise and create a watery layer that softens the toast. I use green leaf lettuce for its gentle crunch and wide, cup-shaped leaves that cradle the bacon. Butter lettuce works too, but it's more delicate. Avoid iceberg — it's too watery and adds little flavor.</p>

<h2>The Assembly: Order Matters</h2>
<p>Toast your bread to a pale gold. Not blond, not dark brown — just dry enough to hold structure without becoming brittle. Let the toast cool for 30 seconds. Warm bread will wilt the lettuce.</p>
<p>Spread mayonnaise on both slices, going edge to edge. This isn't just for flavor; it's a waterproof seal. Use a full tablespoon per slice — enough to taste, not so much that it squishes out. I season the mayonnaise on the bottom slice with a crack of black pepper.</p>
<p>Layer in this order from bottom up: tomato slices, lettuce (folded to fit), bacon. The tomato goes directly on the mayo so its juices are trapped by the fat. The lettuce sits on top of the tomato, creating a buffer that keeps the bacon dry. The bacon goes on top, where its warmth can soften the lettuce just slightly — but not enough to wilt it.</p>
<p>Press the top slice down gently with your palm. Slice diagonally with a serrated knife, using a sawing motion. A straight blade will crush the layers. Serve immediately.</p>

<h2>The Takeaway</h2>
<p>A great BLT doesn't require fancy ingredients or complicated steps. It demands attention to detail: dry tomatoes, dry lettuce, a proper moisture barrier, and bacon cooked with intention. Follow this method once, and you'll never go back to the soggy, uneven version. It's the sandwich of summer — and it deserves to be perfect.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<h3>What kind of bacon is best for a BLT?</h3>
<p>Thick-cut bacon (about 1/8-inch thick) is ideal. It holds its texture against the juicy tomato and doesn't turn to dust when you bite. Thin bacon can become too brittle or, worse, chewy and flabby.</p>
<h3>Can I make a BLT ahead of time?</h3>
<p>No. A BLT is best eaten immediately after assembly. Once the tomato juice hits the bread, it begins to soften the toast. Prep all components separately, then assemble just before serving.</p>
<h3>How do I keep the bread from getting soggy?</h3>
<p>Two tricks: First, spread mayonnaise edge to edge on both slices of toasted bread — the fat creates a waterproof barrier. Second, pat tomato slices dry on a paper towel for 2 minutes before layering.</p>
<h3>What's the best tomato for a BLT?</h3>
<p>A ripe beefsteak or heirloom tomato is ideal. It should be heavy for its size, deeply red, and yield slightly to gentle pressure. Avoid mealy or pale tomatoes — they lack flavor and texture.</p>
<h3>Can I use turkey bacon instead?</h3>
<p>Yes, but the texture will be different. Turkey bacon is leaner and won't crisp as well. Bake it at 375°F for 12-15 minutes, and don't expect the same shatter-crisp bite.</p>
